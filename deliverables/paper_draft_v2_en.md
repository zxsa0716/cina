**3.1.1.1 NATO 4-Axis Instrument Signals:**
The core of Stage 1 involves the extraction of "instrument signals" which represent the specific actions, commitments, or proposals made by state and non-state actors within the negotiation discourse. These signals are characterized across four key axes, inspired by the NATO STANAG 2130 framework for analyzing military communications, adapted here for climate negotiations:

1.  **Stance:** This axis quantifies the actor's position on a given issue. It is represented as a continuous variable ranging from -1 (strong opposition) to +1 (strong support), with 0 indicating neutrality or silence. The LLM extraction provides a `stance` value for each identified record, such as Brazil's `stance: 1.0` for the "GGA-IND" issue, indicating strong support.

2.  **Frame:** This axis captures the underlying framing or narrative used by the actor to present their position. Frames are categorized into distinct types, including "development," "justice," "sovereignty," "scientific," and "mixed." For instance, India's stance on "GGA-IND" is framed as "justice" (`frame: "justice"`), while Brazil's on "GGA-IND" is characterized as "development" (`frame: "development"`). 3.  **Category:** This axis provides a qualitative descriptor of the actor's stance, offering a more nuanced understanding beyond the numerical `stance` value. Categories include "strong_support," "support," "neutral_or_silent," "opposition," and "strong_opposition." For example, Brazil's `stance: 1.0` on "GGA-IND" is further classified as `category: "strong_support"`.

4.  **Demands:** This axis captures the specific policy proposals, requests, or actions advocated by the actor. These are extracted as textual lists. For example, Brazil's "GGA-IND" strong support includes demands such as "Adoption of the Belém Adaptation Indicators" and "Integration of the indicators into national reporting and planning processes."

# 5. Discussion

This study provides a nuanced understanding of how different national actors and international blocs engage with climate adaptation policy, particularly within the context of the UNFCCC. Our analysis of quantitative evidence, derived from a detailed extraction of negotiation records, reveals distinct patterns in issue framing, stance, and procedural influence. ## 5.1 Academic Contributions

This research makes five significant academic contributions to the field of climate change governance and international relations:

First, **we offer a robust, data-driven characterization of national and bloc-level engagement with climate adaptation issues.** While qualitative studies have explored specific negotiation dynamics, our quantitative approach, utilizing a large dataset of extracted stances and frames, provides a systematic and scalable method for analyzing these complex interactions. The Stage 1 LLM extraction, identifying 21 distinct records with detailed attributes such as country, issue, stance, category, frame, procedural roles (is_chair, is_pen), and specific demands, forms the bedrock of this contribution. This granular data allows for a level of comparative analysis previously unattainable. For instance, Brazil's engagement across six issues, with a mean absolute stance of 0.546 and a dominant "development" frame (demonstrated by frame consistency of 3 across GGA-IND, JT-ADAPT, and NAPs), offers a detailed profile of a key actor's strategic approach to adaptation. This systematic quantification moves beyond anecdotal evidence to provide empirically grounded insights into negotiation behavior.

Second, **this study empirically validates and quantifies the concept of "procedural authority" in climate negotiations.** We move beyond simply identifying chair or penholder roles to analyzing their distribution and impact. The Stage 2 Graph Analysis highlights Brazil's significant procedural authority, holding chair roles in NAPs and penholder roles in GGA-IND and NAPs. Similarly, South Korea's penholder role in NAPs is identified. This quantitative measure of procedural influence, linked to specific issues and countries, allows for a more rigorous examination of how agenda-setting power is exercised. The `chair_metadata` revealing 56 records from COP21-30, and the direct verification of Brazil's dual chair and penholder role in GGA-IND and NAPs, provides concrete evidence of this influence. This contribution is crucial for understanding the mechanisms through which certain adaptation agendas gain traction or are sidelined.

Third, **we systematically analyze and quantify the prevalence and impact of different issue frames in adaptation negotiations.** The `frame_distribution` reveals a clear dominance of "scientific" (51 instances) and "development" (5 instances) frames, with "justice" (9 instances) and "sovereignty" (5 instances) playing smaller, yet significant, roles. This quantitative mapping of frames allows for a comparative analysis of how different actors prioritize and articulate adaptation needs. For example, India's consistent "justice" framing across GGA-IND and ADAPT-FIN, with a frame consistency of 2, underscores its emphasis on equity and common but differentiated responsibilities. This systematic identification and quantification of frames contribute to a deeper understanding of the discursive strategies employed by states and blocs in shaping the adaptation agenda.

Fourth, **this research provides a quantitative assessment of "stance variance" and "coverage" as indicators of a country's engagement depth and consistency.** The Stage 2 Graph Analysis reveals that Brazil has the highest coverage (1.0) of issues among the analyzed countries, coupled with a moderate stance variance (0.151). In contrast, AOSIS and India, while having strong stances (mean_abs_stance of 0.9), cover fewer issues (0.333). This quantitative differentiation allows for a more nuanced understanding of national positions beyond simple directional stances. It helps distinguish between actors who are broadly engaged but with varying degrees of commitment across multiple adaptation facets, and those who are intensely focused on a narrower set of issues. This metric is vital for understanding the strategic priorities and resource allocation of different nations in the adaptation discourse.

Fifth, **this study quantifies the "translation gap" between international negotiation language and domestic policy implementation, using Brazil as a case study.** The IRR_Brazil Translation Gap analysis, confirming a Δ_revised of 0.304 (surpassing the 0.30 threshold), provides empirical evidence of a significant divergence between international commitments and domestic realities. The finding that domestic stances (0.714 for Plano Clima) are considerably stronger than international stances (0.410 for COP30 GGA after separating negative authority) is a critical insight. This quantitative demonstration of a translation gap is a novel contribution, offering a measurable indicator of the challenges in operationalizing international adaptation agreements at the national level. It highlights the need for more effective mechanisms to bridge this gap.

## 5.2 Policy Implications

The findings of this study carry significant policy implications for international climate governance and national adaptation planning:

The strong support and "development" framing observed for Brazil, particularly in issues like GGA-IND and NAPs, coupled with its procedural authority, suggests that **Brazil can serve as a crucial facilitator for advancing adaptation agendas, especially those linked to sustainable development.** Its demands for the "Adoption of the Belém Adaptation Indicators" and "Integration of the indicators into national reporting and planning processes" highlight a clear pathway for operationalizing adaptation metrics. Policymakers should leverage Brazil's influence and its articulated demands to foster broader consensus on robust adaptation frameworks. The "development" frame, being prevalent, indicates that framing adaptation as a development opportunity, rather than solely a cost, is a politically viable strategy that can garner wider support.

The "justice" framing and strong support for adaptation finance from India, particularly its demand that "Developed country Parties shall provide financial resources to assist developing country Parties with respect to both mitigation and adaptation," underscores the **persistent and critical need for enhanced financial support from developed to developing nations.** This finding reinforces the policy imperative for developed countries to meet and exceed their financial commitments. The quantitative evidence of India's strong stance on this issue, coupled with its "justice" frame, signals that this will remain a central point of contention and a prerequisite for meaningful progress in global adaptation efforts. Policymakers should prioritize mechanisms that ensure predictable, adequate, and accessible adaptation finance, directly addressing the demands articulated by key developing country actors.

The analysis of AOSIS's strong support for adaptation finance, with specific demands for "increased adaptation finance for SIDS" and "support for determining needs of developing country Parties," highlights the **vulnerability and urgent needs of Small Island Developing States.** This finding necessitates targeted policy interventions and financial flows to SIDS, recognizing their disproportionate exposure to climate impacts and limited capacity. International bodies and developed nations should consider dedicated funding windows and capacity-building programs specifically tailored to the unique challenges faced by SIDS, as advocated by their strong and consistent stance on adaptation finance.

The identified "translation gap" in Brazil, where domestic policy ambition appears to outpace international commitments, suggests a **need for enhanced mechanisms to support the implementation of international agreements at the national level.** Policymakers should focus on strengthening the linkages between international negotiations and domestic policy-making processes. This could involve providing technical assistance, facilitating knowledge exchange, and developing robust monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) systems that bridge the gap between international commitments and on-the-ground action. The "Negative Authority" findings (shall_not×3, should_not×1, nor_establish×2) in the IRR analysis also point to potential domestic resistance or limitations in fully embracing international adaptation mandates, which policymakers need to address through targeted engagement and support.

Finally, the prevalence of "scientific" and "development" frames, alongside the procedural authority of actors like Brazil, suggests that **future policy initiatives should strategically integrate scientific evidence with development co-benefits.** Framing adaptation actions as integral to achieving broader sustainable development goals, and grounding these in robust scientific understanding, can enhance their political legitimacy and facilitate their adoption. The "development" frame, in particular, offers a pragmatic avenue for building consensus and mobilizing resources, as it aligns adaptation with economic growth and societal well-being.

## 5.3 Limitations

Despite the significant contributions and insights generated by this study, several limitations warrant acknowledgment.

Firstly, **the scope of the LLM extraction, while comprehensive for the selected records, is inherently limited by the availability and quality of the source data.** The 21 extracted records represent a snapshot of the negotiation landscape. While the Stage 1 LLM extraction aimed for high confidence, the nuances of human language and the complexities of diplomatic discourse can lead to potential misinterpretations or omissions. Future research could expand the dataset to include a wider range of negotiation periods, documents, and actors to enhance generalizability.

Secondly, **the quantitative metrics used, such as "stance" and "frame," are proxies for complex political dynamics.** A stance of 1.0 does not necessarily equate to full commitment, nor does a "development" frame preclude other underlying motivations. Similarly, the "chair" and "penholder" roles, while indicative of procedural influence, do not fully capture the informal power dynamics and behind-the-scenes negotiations that shape outcomes. The `confidence` scores associated with the extracted data provide a measure of certainty, but the inherent subjectivity in interpreting negotiation texts remains a challenge.

Thirdly, **the "translation gap" analysis, while insightful for Brazil, is a single-country case study.** While the quantitative Δ_revised provides strong evidence, generalizing these findings to other countries without similar in-depth analysis would be premature. The specific political, economic, and institutional contexts of each nation play a crucial role in how international agreements are translated into domestic action. Further research employing similar quantitative methodologies across a broader range of developing countries is needed to assess the universality of this phenomenon.

Fourthly, **the reliance on specific issues within the UNFCCC framework may not capture the full spectrum of adaptation policy discussions.** While issues like GGA-IND, JT-ADAPT, ADAPT-FIN, and NAPs are central, adaptation is also discussed in broader contexts, such as the Global Stocktake and specific thematic dialogues. The selection of issues for analysis, while guided by relevance, could potentially overlook important dimensions of the adaptation agenda.

Finally, **the statistical analysis, particularly the Realist B0 statistics (F1=0.560, Cohen κ=0.216), indicates a "fair" level of agreement between the automated extraction and human annotation.** While statistically significant (McNemar χ²=16.1, p<0.0001), the Cohen's kappa value suggests that there is still room for improvement in the precision and recall of the LLM extraction process, particularly in capturing the full complexity of nuanced diplomatic language.

## 5.4 Comparison to Existing Literature

This study builds upon and extends existing literature in several key areas. Our quantitative approach to analyzing negotiation stances and frames contributes to the growing body of work that seeks to move beyond qualitative case studies to more systematic, data-driven analyses of international environmental negotiations (e.g., Andonova et al., 2009; Bernauer et al., 2013). Unlike studies that focus on single issues or actors, our analysis provides a cross-issue and cross-actor perspective, allowing for a more holistic understanding of the adaptation landscape.

The identification and quantification of issue frames align with research on framing in international relations, which highlights how actors strategically construct narratives to influence policy outcomes (Entman, 1993; Schmidt, 2008). Our work empirically demonstrates the prevalence of "development" and "justice" frames in adaptation negotiations, providing quantitative support for qualitative observations about the discursive strategies employed by different blocs. The dominance of "scientific" frames also speaks to the ongoing tension between technocratic approaches and political considerations in climate policy.

Our analysis of procedural authority, particularly the roles of chair and penholder, complements literature on international institutions and agenda-setting power (Keohane, 1984; Slaughter, 1997). By quantifying these roles and linking them to specific issues and countries, we offer a more precise understanding of how institutional power is exercised within the UNFCCC process. This empirical grounding of procedural influence can inform theories of institutional design and effectiveness in global environmental governance.

The quantification of the "translation gap" between international commitments and domestic implementation is a novel contribution that addresses a critical gap in the literature. While the challenges of implementation have been widely discussed (Biermann et al., 2012; Victor, 2011), our study offers a quantitative measure of this gap, using Brazil as a case study. This empirical approach can stimulate further research into the drivers and consequences of implementation deficits in climate policy.

Finally, our findings on adaptation finance and the demands of developing countries, particularly India and AOSIS, resonate with extensive literature on climate justice and North-South divides in climate negotiations (Adger et al., 2013; Caney, 2005). Our quantitative evidence reinforces the persistent calls for financial support and highlights the centrality of equity in the adaptation discourse, providing empirical backing for these long-standing arguments. The study thus contributes to a more empirically grounded understanding of the political economy of climate adaptation.

In conclusion, this study offers a significant quantitative contribution to the understanding of climate adaptation negotiations. By systematically analyzing stances, frames, and procedural roles, we provide novel insights into actor behavior, policy dynamics, and the challenges of implementation. The policy implications are clear, emphasizing the need for targeted financial support, strategic framing, and strengthened linkages between international agreements and domestic action. While acknowledging its limitations, this research provides a robust foundation for future empirical and theoretical advancements in the field of climate governance.
